# Speaker Notes – Mathematical and Probabilistic Models in *Provably Secure Steganography*

**Based on:** Nicholas J. Hopper, John Langford, and Luis von Ahn, *Provably Secure Steganography* (2002)

---

## Slide 1 – Title

### What to say

Good morning everyone. Today I am presenting a literature review of the paper *Provably Secure Steganography* by Nicholas J. Hopper, John Langford, and Luis von Ahn, published in 2002.

The main focus of my review is not only steganography itself, but the **mathematical and probabilistic models** used to formally describe secure steganographic communication.

In particular, I will explain how the paper models the communication channel as a probability distribution, how random variables and entropy are used, how rejection sampling allows information to be hidden, and how probability is used to define security and robustness.

---

# Slide 2 – Problem Statement

### What to say

The basic problem is how Alice can send a secret message to Bob without a warden realizing that secret communication is taking place.

This is different from traditional cryptography.

Cryptography generally tries to hide the **content** of a message. Steganography tries to hide the **existence of the message itself**.

The paper considers a communication channel that is publicly observable by a warden. The warden may monitor the communication and try to determine whether Alice and Bob are using steganography.

The important contribution of this paper is that it does not treat steganography only as a practical technique. Instead, it gives a **formal, complexity-theoretic definition of security**.

The central question is:

> Can we construct a steganographic system whose output is computationally indistinguishable from ordinary communication over the channel?

The paper also considers **robust steganography**, where the warden is allowed to modify the communication in an attempt to destroy the hidden message.

---

# Slide 3 – Communication Channel as a Probability Distribution

### What to say

The first important mathematical idea is the model of the communication channel.

The paper represents a channel as a **distribution over sequences of timestamped bits**.

For example, we can think about an email channel. An email contains many bits, and the sequence of emails produced by normal users can be viewed as samples from some probability distribution.

The important point is that the channel is not deterministic. There is randomness in what normal communication looks like.

The paper represents a partial channel draw using:

$$
X_h \sim C_h^b
$$

Here:

* \(h\) represents the communication history.
* \(b\) represents the block size.
* \(C_h^b\) represents the distribution of the next block given the previous history.
* \(X_h\) represents an actual random sample from that distribution.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
C_h^b =
P(\text{next }b\text{ bits}\mid\text{previous history }h)
}
$$

This conditional probability model is important because what appears next in a communication channel can depend on what has already happened.

# Slide 4 – Random Variables and Their Distributions

### What to put on the slide

| Random variable / object | Meaning                      | Distribution                        |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| \(H\)                    | Previous channel history     | Generated by the channel            |
| \(C_h^b\)                | Next \(b\)-bit channel block | \(X_h\sim C_h^b\)                   |
| \(C\)                    | A sampled channel block      | \(C\sim C_h^b\)                     |
| \(M\)                    | Hidden message               | Input to the encoder                |
| \(K\)                    | Secret key                   | \(K\sim U(k)\)                      |
| \(F_K\)                  | PRF using key \(K\)          | Compared with random function \(g\) |
| \(W\)                    | Warden's decision            | \(W\in\{0,1\}\)                     |
| \(T\)                    | Samples until acceptance     | \(T=\min\{i:F(C_i)=M_i\}\)          |

### Key model

$$
H=h
\longrightarrow
C_h^b
\longrightarrow
C
\longrightarrow
F(C)
\longrightarrow
\text{Rejection Sampling}
$$

### What to say

Before discussing entropy and rejection sampling, it is important to identify exactly **what is random in the paper's model**.

The most important random object is the **channel block**.

If \(h\) represents the communication history, then:

$$
X_h\sim C_h^b
$$

means that \(X_h\) is a random block of \(b\) timestamped bits drawn from the channel, given the previous history.

The paper therefore does **not** simply assume that every channel bit is independently and identically distributed. The distribution of the next block can depend on the communication history.

We can think of the process as:

$$
H\rightarrow C_h^b\rightarrow C
$$

where \(H\) represents what has already happened, \(C_h^b\) represents the conditional distribution of the next block, and \(C\) is an actual sample drawn from that distribution.

The next important object is the **secret key**.

The paper samples the key uniformly:

$$
K\leftarrow U(k)
$$

so every \(k\)-bit key is equally likely.

The hidden message \(M\) is slightly different.

The paper treats the message primarily as an **input to the encoding algorithm**, rather than specifying a probability distribution for the message itself.

During rejection sampling, the encoder repeatedly obtains random channel samples:

$$
C_1,C_2,C_3,\ldots
$$

and checks them against the required condition.

We can define a useful random variable \(T\) as the **number of samples required before one is accepted**:

$$
T=\min\{i:F(C_i)=M_i\}.
$$

This helps us understand both the probability of successful encoding and the efficiency of the stegosystem.

Finally, the warden can be represented by a binary random output:

$$
W\in\{0,1\}.
$$

For example:

$$
W=1
$$

can represent the warden deciding that hidden communication is present.

Security is then based on comparing the warden's probability of making this decision for stegotext with the probability of making the same decision for ordinary channel traffic.

# Slide 5 – Entropy and Randomness

### What to say

Once we have identified the random variables, the next question is:

**How much randomness does the communication channel contain?**

The paper answers this using **min-entropy**.

The minimum entropy requirement is:

$$
\boxed{
\forall h:\quad H_\infty(C_h^b)>1
}
$$

For a distribution \(X\), min-entropy is:

$$
H_\infty(X)
=
-\log_2\left(\max_x P[X=x]\right).
$$

Therefore:

$$
H_\infty(C_h^b)>1
$$

means that, even after conditioning on the history, the next block cannot be too predictable.

If the most probable channel block has probability \(p_{\max}\), then:

$$
H_\infty(X)=-\log_2(p_{\max}).
$$

So the condition \(H_\infty(X)>1\) implies:

$$
p_{\max}<\frac12.
$$

This assumption is crucial because the steganographic algorithms repeatedly sample from the channel and use those samples to encode information.

---

# Slide 6 – The Stegosystem

### What to say

The paper defines a stegosystem as:

$$
S=(SE,SD)
$$

where:

* \(SE\) is the encoder.
* \(SD\) is the decoder.

The encoder takes a secret message, a secret key, the communication history, and access to the channel distribution.

It then produces ordinary-looking channel data containing the hidden message.

The decoder uses the secret key and the received stegotext to recover the hidden message.

The paper also defines correctness probabilistically.

For allowed message lengths, the probability that the decoder correctly recovers the hidden message must be at least:

$$
\frac23.
$$

The probability comes from the randomness of the encoder, decoder, and channel sampling.

The paper also notes that error-correcting codes can be used to reduce errors.

---

# Slide 7 – Rejection Sampling

### What to say

The central technique used by the paper is **rejection sampling**.

The idea is simple.

Instead of directly changing a channel sample to encode a secret bit, the encoder repeatedly samples naturally occurring channel blocks until it finds one that satisfies a particular condition.

For example, suppose we want to encode a bit \(m_i\).

The encoder samples a channel block \(c\) and checks:

$$
F_K(N,c)=m_i.
$$

If the condition is not satisfied, the sample is rejected and another channel block is drawn.

If the condition is satisfied, the sample is accepted and transmitted.

So the process looks like:

$$
C_1,C_2,C_3,\ldots
$$

until:

$$
F_K(N,C_i)=m_i.
$$

The important advantage is that the transmitted block is an actual sample from the channel rather than an artificially modified object.

---

# Slide 8 – Probability of Successful Sampling

### What to say

The probabilistic analysis of rejection sampling is one of the important mathematical parts of the paper.

Suppose two independent channel samples are \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), and define:

$$
D=\{X_1\neq X_2\}.
$$

The paper derives:

$$
P[F(SE,N)=0]
=
\frac12+\frac14P[D].
$$

The probability that two samples are different depends on the distribution of the channel.

If the most probable channel symbol has probability \(p_i\), then the collision probability is bounded by that value.

Therefore:

$$
P[D]\geq1-p_i.
$$

Because the minimum entropy assumption gives:

$$
p_i<\frac12,
$$

we obtain:

$$
P[D]>\frac12.
$$

Therefore, the probability of successfully producing the required bit is greater than:

$$
\frac58.
$$

The important point is that **the entropy assumption directly gives us a probabilistic guarantee about the encoder's success**.

---

# Slide 9 – Construction 1: Stateful Steganography

### What to say

The first construction is a **stateful steganographic construction**.

It uses a pseudorandom function, or PRF, together with a counter.

For each hidden message bit, the encoder repeatedly samples a channel block until:

$$
F_K(N,c)=m_i.
$$

Here:

* \(K\) is the secret key.
* \(N\) is the current counter value.
* \(c\) is the channel sample.
* \(m_i\) is the hidden message bit.

The counter is important because it gives the encoder and decoder shared state.

The security of this construction is connected to the security of the pseudorandom function.

If the PRF is computationally indistinguishable from a truly random function, then the warden should not be able to distinguish the steganographic communication from ordinary channel communication except with negligible probability.

---

# Slide 10 – Construction 2: Stateless Steganography

### What to say

The second construction removes the need for state.

Instead of maintaining a counter, it uses encryption together with an unbiased function \(f\).

For a binary function to be unbiased:

$$
P[f(X)=0]
=
P[f(X)=1]
=
\frac12.
$$

The secret message is first encrypted.

Then, for each encrypted bit \(s_i\), the encoder samples channel blocks until it finds one satisfying:

$$
f(c_i)=s_i.
$$

The advantage of an unbiased function is that each desired bit occurs with probability one-half.

The paper also discusses what happens when the function is only approximately unbiased. In that case, the small statistical bias contributes to the insecurity of the construction.

---

# Slide 11 – Computational Security

### What to say

Now we come to the main security definition in the paper.

The paper models the warden as a **probabilistic adversary**.

The warden observes the communication and tries to determine whether it is ordinary communication or whether steganography is being used.

The basic idea is to compare two situations:

1. The warden observes ordinary channel communication.
2. The warden observes communication produced by the steganographic encoder.

The warden's distinguishing advantage can be expressed conceptually as:

$$
\left|
P[W^{SE}=1]
-
P[W^O=1]
\right|.
$$

Here:

* \(W^{SE}\) represents the warden interacting with the steganographic encoder.
* \(W^O\) represents the warden interacting with the ordinary channel.

If these probabilities are almost the same, then the warden cannot reliably tell the two situations apart.

The paper defines security using **computational indistinguishability**.

This means that for any efficient warden, the distinguishing advantage should be **negligible**.

A negligible function is one that becomes smaller than:

$$
\frac{1}{n^c}
$$

for every constant \(c>0\), when \(n\) becomes sufficiently large.

Therefore, security does not mean that the probability of detection is exactly zero.

Instead, it means that an efficient adversary's advantage becomes so small that it is negligible.

---

# Slide 12 – Why the PRF Provides Security

### What to say

The security of Construction 1 is connected to the security of the **pseudorandom function**, or PRF.

A PRF takes a secret key and an input and produces an output that should look random to anyone who does not know the key.

The paper compares two possibilities.

In the first case, the encoder uses:

$$
F_K.
$$

In the second case, the function is replaced by a truly random function:

$$
g.
$$

The security assumption says that an efficient adversary should not be able to distinguish \(F_K\) from \(g\) except with negligible advantage.

This gives the stegosystem its security.

Intuitively, the warden sees channel samples selected according to a function that appears random.

Therefore, the warden should not be able to identify a meaningful pattern connecting the selected channel samples to the hidden message.

The paper formally connects the insecurity of the stegosystem to the insecurity of the PRF.

So the main relationship is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{PRF security}
\Rightarrow
\text{Steganographic security}
}
$$

This is important because the authors can prove security using an established cryptographic assumption rather than relying only on experimental observations.

---

# Slide 13 – Robustness Against an Active Warden

### What to say

Security against a passive warden is not the only problem.

The paper also considers an **active warden** who can modify the communication.

To model this, the paper introduces a relation:

$$
R\subseteq X\times Y.
$$

This relation describes which modifications an attacker is allowed to make.

The paper defines the **obfuscation probability** as:

$$
O(R,D)
=
\max_y
\sum_{(x',y)\in R}P_D[x'].
$$

This measures how much probability mass from the original distribution can be mapped to the same output by the warden.

The relation is called \(\delta\)-admissible when:

$$
O(R,C_h^b)\leq\delta.
$$

Informally, robustness depends on limiting how effectively the warden can modify channel samples in a way that interferes with the hidden communication.

---

# Slide 14 – Random Variables, Martingales, and Azuma's Inequality

### What to say

The robustness proof introduces another useful probabilistic model.

The paper defines indicator random variables:

$$
X_i=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if the warden alters the relevant shingle}\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

These variables allow the authors to count how many relevant parts of the stegotext have been affected.

They then construct the sequence:

$$
Y_0=
E\left[\sum X_i\right]
$$

and

$$
Y_j=
E\left[
\sum X_i
\mid
X_1,\ldots,X_j
\right].
$$

This forms a **martingale sequence**.

The authors then use **Azuma's inequality** to bound the probability that the actual number of modifications deviates significantly from its expected value.

Therefore, the robustness argument also becomes a probability problem:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Expected modifications}
+
\text{concentration bound}
\Rightarrow
\text{robustness guarantee}
}
$$

This demonstrates that probability theory is used not only to construct the steganographic signal, but also to prove that the communication remains reliable when an active adversary interferes with it.

---

# Slide 15 – Overall Mathematical Model

### What to say

We can now put the different mathematical components together.

The paper starts with a **probabilistic channel model**:

$$
X_h\sim C_h^b.
$$

The channel needs sufficient randomness, expressed through:

$$
H_\infty(C_h^b)>1.
$$

The encoder then uses **rejection sampling** to select channel samples that represent the secret information.

The security of the selection process is supported by a **pseudorandom function**.

Security is measured using the difference between the warden's probabilities of identifying steganographic and ordinary communication:

$$
\left|
P[W^{SE}=1]
-
P[W^O=1]
\right|.
$$

For active attacks, the paper introduces relations, indicator random variables, martingales, and Azuma's inequality to establish robustness.

Therefore, the paper combines several mathematical ideas:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Probability}
+
\text{Entropy}
+
\text{Random Variables}
+
\text{Rejection Sampling}
+
\text{Pseudorandomness}
+
\text{Concentration Bounds}
}
$$

Together, these provide a formal mathematical foundation for secure steganography.

---

# Slide 16 – Main Findings and Conclusion

### What to say

To conclude, the main contribution of the paper is that it moves steganography from an intuitive or mainly heuristic concept toward a **formal security framework**.

The communication channel is treated as a probability distribution rather than as a fixed object.

Entropy provides the randomness needed for hiding information.

Rejection sampling allows the encoder to select natural channel outputs without directly modifying them.

Pseudorandom functions provide the computational security mechanism.

Finally, probability theory is used to analyze both the success of encoding and the robustness of the system against an active warden.

The paper's main theoretical result is that secure and robust steganographic protocols can be constructed under standard cryptographic assumptions, particularly the existence of **one-way functions**.

So the key idea from this literature review is:

> **Steganographic security can be expressed and analyzed mathematically by modelling normal communication probabilistically and then proving that steganographic communication is computationally indistinguishable from that normal distribution.**

The central message of the review is therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Channel randomness}
\rightarrow
\text{Rejection sampling}
\rightarrow
\text{Stegotext}
\rightarrow
\text{Security and robustness proofs}
}
$$

---

# Final Takeaway

If I had to summarize the entire review in one sentence:

> **The paper uses probability, entropy, pseudorandomness, and statistical concentration to mathematically model how secret information can be hidden in ordinary communication while remaining difficult to detect or disrupt.**
